Designing a Wi-Fi Smoker Fan Controller

A first look at the electronics, control strategy, and safety decisions behind a small controller for holding a smoker at temperature.

The idea

I want to build a small device that can help hold a charcoal or wood smoker at a target temperature. The basic idea is simple: measure the air temperature inside the smoker, control the amount of combustion air with a blower, and make the current state visible over a local Wi-Fi page.

This is not meant to be a gas-valve controller or a mains-powered heater. It is a combustion-air controller that adds air when the fire needs help and stops adding air when it does not. The fire still has thermal inertia and natural draft, so turning the fan off cannot make the temperature drop immediately.

The first build

I am deliberately starting with assembled development boards instead of jumping straight to a custom PCB. The first bench prototype is built around four main pieces:

  • A Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-C3 for the control logic, Wi-Fi, and local web interface.
  • An Adafruit MAX31856 breakout to read an ungrounded K-type thermocouple.
  • A suitable 12 V blower and a default-off MOSFET driver to switch it.
  • A small buck converter to make 5 V for the XIAO when the prototype moves from separate USB and 12 V supplies to one 12 V adapter.

The modules keep the early work focused on the parts that are hardest to get right: choosing a blower, understanding its startup behavior, getting reliable temperature readings, and deciding how the control loop should behave. A solderless breadboard is fine for signals, but the fan current will stay in properly sized wiring and connectors rather than passing through breadboard contacts.

How the pieces fit together

A regulated 12 V input feeds the blower branch and the lower-voltage electronics. The ESP32 drives the MOSFET gate, while the blower remains electrically separate from the sensor circuitry as much as possible. The MAX31856 talks to the ESP32 over SPI and handles the small thermocouple signal, cold-junction compensation, and sensor fault reporting.

The initial fan is a two-wire, 12 V blower controlled with slow on/off decisions. That is an important constraint: a PWM-capable MOSFET does not automatically make an arbitrary two-wire blower compatible with supply PWM. The first version will use full-voltage switching with hysteresis and minimum on/off times. If smooth speed control or stall feedback becomes necessary, I will select a documented four-wire blower and design its PWM and tachometer interface around that specific model.

Safety is part of the design

A network connection cannot be a requirement for safe temperature control. The control loop should continue locally when Wi-Fi or the router is unavailable, and it should never wait for a web request before making its next decision. Wi-Fi is for settings, monitoring, and alarms—not for keeping the fire-control loop alive.

The intended default is fan off. The hardware should hold the fan off during reset and startup, and the firmware should stop it for an open probe, thermocouple-converter fault, stale measurement, overtemperature, or local STOP command. A physical fan disconnect remains useful even if the software is broken. These measures do not turn the project into a certified unattended safety controller; they make the failure behavior explicit and testable.

From prototype to PCB

Once the blower and sensor behavior are understood, the next step is a small custom board around an ESP32-C3 module, a 3.3 V buck converter, a MAX31856, and the verified fan-driver circuit. The layout matters as much as the schematic: the thermocouple connector and converter need a quiet, thermally stable area, while the fan current and buck switching loops need short, deliberate power paths. The antenna also needs its keepout, especially if the electronics eventually live near a metal smoker.

The board is not the whole system. The blower needs an adapter and a way to limit uncontrolled airflow when it is off. The enclosure needs protection from rain, grease, ash, and heat. The probe, cable, connector, fuse, wiring, and power supply all need ratings that match the real installation.

What I am trying to learn

The project is as much about learning the boundary between software and hardware as it is about making a useful smoker accessory. I want to measure the real electrical behavior instead of trusting nominal current ratings, understand how fan switching affects temperature readings, and find a control strategy that behaves sensibly around lid openings, fuel changes, and the slow response of a smoker.

The build sequence is intentionally conservative: manual fan testing, then temperature hysteresis, then a more capable controller only if the measurements justify it. Before any supervised smoker trials, I need to test probe failures, resets, power cycling, Wi-Fi loss, fan startup, and the cases where a sensor can return a plausible but incorrect reading.

For now, this is a design proposal rather than a finished product. The first milestone is not a polished PCB—it is a small, well-understood prototype that can control airflow predictably and fail with the fan off.
